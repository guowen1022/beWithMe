Kung fu, properly read

Less a sport than a thousand-year argument about what a body is for.

The teacher is going to attempt a thing that the genre called "explainer" rarely attempts — to say what kung fu is, rather than what it does.1 The two are not the same. To say what it does is to list its motions, its competitions, its films. To say what it is requires a longer breath and an older book.

1The phrasing borrows from Stanley Cavell, who insisted that the question what is film admits a different kind of answer than what does film do.

When the monks at the Shaolin temple first systematized what we now call gong fu (功夫) in the seventh century, they were not inventing a martial art. They were inventing a way to inhabit a body that did not flinch2 — a body that could stand thirty years of dawn practice, twenty of standing meditation, and still arrive at sixty more alert, not less. The combat was always a byproduct.

2Wu shu (武术) is the modern PRC competition form — dazzling, but a different species from the temple practice it descends from.

The two minds

There is a distinction we inherit from Tang-era manuals between wu shu (武术) — the art of combat — and gong fu — the labor of perfecting any practice worth perfecting.3 A cook can have gong fu. A calligrapher can have gong fu. The fighter's gong fu is one species inside a much larger genus.

3Hua Tuo, third-century physician, is the earliest figure recorded as teaching exercises (the wǔ qín xì, or "five animal frolics") for health and longevity rather than combat.

This is why the translation "Chinese martial arts" mis-aims. It locks the term inside one room of a much larger house. The house's other rooms — calligraphy, tea, medicine — belong to the same lineage of practice as moral instrument, and the moment you let them back in, the whole subject changes shape.

The lineage problem

Every claimed lineage in kung fu eventually meets the same wall: the records were burned, the temples sacked, the masters scattered.4 What survives is a chain of remembered teaching — student to student, often across centuries of disruption. The historian's verdict is usually "we cannot confirm before 1700." The practitioner's verdict is "and yet here is the form, and it works."

4The Qing dynasty's repeated suppression of southern Shaolin lineages in the eighteenth century is the canonical example — evidence and counter-evidence both survive, in roughly equal volume.

For a reading assistant whose job is to fit explanation to a particular reader, this is the interesting tension: kung fu is a domain where the lived experience of the lineage is more trustworthy than the written record of it. Most domains are the opposite — and that asymmetry is, if you let it, the whole shape of the subject.

Five families

The five so-called family styles of southern kung fu — Hung, Lau, Choy, Lei, Mok — are not really five different martial arts. They are five different answers to the same question: given a body that must defend a village while also farming, weaving, and aging, what is the smallest set of motions worth memorizing for life?

Read this way, the styles stop competing and start completing. Hung Gar is the answer if your terrain is mountainous; Wing Chun is the answer if your fight is in a doorway; Tai Chi Chuan is the answer if your fight is with the next sixty years of your own joints. None of them is the kung fu. The kung fu is the shape of the question itself.
